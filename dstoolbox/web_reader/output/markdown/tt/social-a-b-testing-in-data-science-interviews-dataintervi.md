What is A/B Testing? (in data science interviews)
👋 Let's learn together ↓
A/B testing is a 𝘀𝘁𝗮𝘁𝗶𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗮𝗹 𝗺𝗲𝘁𝗵𝗼𝗱 𝘁𝗼 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗽𝗮𝗿𝗲 𝘁𝘄𝗼 𝘃𝗮𝗿𝗶𝗮𝗻𝘁𝘀 and determine which performs better on a target metric.
You split users randomly into control (A) and treatment (B) groups, expose them to different versions, then measure if the difference in outcomes is real or just noise.
It's how companies decide whether a new feature actually improves conversion, retention, or revenue.
📐 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝘁𝗲𝘀𝘁 𝘀𝘁𝗮𝘁𝗶𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗰:
Z = (p̂ᵦ - p̂ₐ) / sqrt(p̄(1-p̄)(1/nₐ + 1/nᵦ))
Where:
p̂ₐ, p̂ᵦ → observed conversion rates in each group
p̄ → pooled proportion under null hypothesis
nₐ, nᵦ → sample sizes per group
💪 𝗛𝗼𝘄 𝗶𝘁 𝘄𝗼𝗿𝗸𝘀:
① Define your metric and minimum detectable effect
② Calculate required sample size based on α (Type I error) and power
③ Randomly assign users to control or treatment
④ Run the test until you hit sample size
⑤ Compute Z-score and compare to critical value
⑥ Reject null if |Z| exceeds threshold, otherwise fail to reject
🧐 𝗛𝗼𝘄 𝗶𝘀 𝗶𝘁 𝗱𝗶𝗳𝗳𝗲𝗿𝗲𝗻𝘁 𝗳𝗿𝗼𝗺 𝗕𝗮𝘆𝗲𝘀𝗶𝗮𝗻 𝗔/𝗕?
Classical A/B gives you a binary decision (reject or not) based on p-values and requires fixed sample sizes.
Bayesian A/B gives you a probability distribution over the treatment effect, lets you stop early when confident, and directly answers "what's the chance B is better?"
Classical is easier to explain to stakeholders. Bayesian is more flexible but needs prior specification.
✍️ 𝗪𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝘁𝗼 𝘂𝘀𝗲 𝗔/𝗕 𝘁𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴:
when you need to validate product changes with statistical confidence before rolling them out to everyone.

  What is Bayesian Inference? (in Data interviews)
  👋 Let's learn together ↓
  𝗕𝗮𝘆𝗲𝘀𝗶𝗮𝗻 𝗶𝗻𝗳𝗲𝗿𝗲𝗻𝗰𝗲 𝗶𝘀 𝗮 𝗺𝗲𝘁𝗵𝗼𝗱 𝗳𝗼𝗿 𝘂𝗽𝗱𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗯𝗲𝗹𝗶𝗲𝗳𝘀 𝗮𝗯𝗼𝘂𝘁 𝗽𝗮𝗿𝗮𝗺𝗲𝘁𝗲𝗿𝘀 𝗮𝘀 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝘀𝗲𝗲 𝗻𝗲𝘄 𝗱𝗮𝘁𝗮.
  You start with a prior belief (what you think before seeing data), then combine it with the likelihood (what the data tells you) to get a posterior belief (your updated understanding).
  More data means sharper, more confident posteriors. Less data means your prior has more influence.
  📐 𝗕𝗮𝘆𝗲𝘀' 𝗥𝘂𝗹𝗲:
  P(θ | D) = P(D | θ) P(θ) / P(D)
  Where:
  P(θ | D) → posterior (belief after seeing data)
  P(D | θ) → likelihood (how probable the data is given θ)
  P(θ) → prior (belief before seeing data)
  P(D) → evidence (normalizing constant)
  ⚡ 𝗛𝗼𝘄 𝗶𝘁 𝘄𝗼𝗿𝗸𝘀:
  ① Choose a prior distribution for your parameter
  ② Observe data and compute the likelihood
  ③ Apply Bayes' rule to get the posterior distribution
  ④ Use the posterior as your new prior for future updates
  🧐 𝗛𝗼𝘄 𝗶𝘀 𝗶𝘁 𝗱𝗶𝗳𝗳𝗲𝗿𝗲𝗻𝘁 𝗳𝗿𝗼𝗺 𝗙𝗿𝗲𝗾𝘂𝗲𝗻𝘁𝗶𝘀𝘁 𝗶𝗻𝗳𝗲𝗿𝗲𝗻𝗰𝗲?
  Frequentist methods treat parameters as fixed unknowns and rely on repeated sampling. You get point estimates and confidence intervals.
  Bayesian methods treat parameters as random variables with distributions. You get full probability distributions over parameters and can incorporate prior knowledge naturally.
  Frequentist says "if we repeated this experiment many times..." while Bayesian says "given this data, here's what I believe."
  ✍️ 𝗪𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝘁𝗼 𝘂𝘀𝗲 𝗕𝗮𝘆𝗲𝘀𝗶𝗮𝗻 𝗶𝗻𝗳𝗲𝗿𝗲𝗻𝗰𝗲:
  when you have prior knowledge to incorporate, need uncertainty estimates, or want to update beliefs sequentially as new data arrives.

  📊 Data Profiling: The Essential First Step Toward Reliable Data Science & AI
  Before building models, creating dashboards, or generating insights, one critical step should never be overlooked is data profiling.
  Data profiling is the process of examining each variable in a dataset to understand its characteristics, quality, and structure. It helps data scientists gain a comprehensive understanding of the data before performing any analysis or machine learning tasks.
  🔎 Why is Data Profiling Important?
  ✅ Understand Sample Size;
  The number of observations directly impacts the reliability of conclusions.
  Larger datasets generally provide more robust statistical insights and better represent the target population.
  Small datasets may reveal misleading patterns, while large-scale datasets often require specialized big data processing techniques.
  ✅ Analyze Basic Statistics:
  Metrics such as mean, median, mode, standard deviation, and range help reveal the distribution and variability of data.
  These statistics provide an initial understanding of trends, anomalies, and potential data quality issues.
  ✅ Assess Data Completeness
  Missing or null values can significantly affect analytical outcomes.
  Profiling helps identify incomplete variables and supports decisions on whether to:
  ✔️Remove missing records
  ✔️Exclude variables
  ✔️Apply data imputation techniques
  🔎 The Real Value of Data Profiling: Data profiling helps uncover:
  ✔️ Outliers and anomalies
  ✔️ Missing values
  ✔️ Inconsistent data types
  ✔️ Distribution patterns
  ✔️ Data quality issues before they become modeling problems
  🔎 Tools That Make Profiling Easier: Python libraries such as pandas-profiling and ydata-profiling can automatically generate comprehensive profiling reports, including:
  ✔️Variable distributions
  ✔️Missing value analysis
  ✔️Correlation matrices
  ✔️Data type summaries
  ✔️Data quality assessments
  These tools enable data professionals to quickly identify issues and gain valuable insights at scale.
  Remember: The quality of your insights is directly tied to the quality of your data. Data profiling is not just a preliminary step, it is the foundation of trustworthy analytics and successful machine learning projects.

  "Will AI replace the analyst?" is the wrong question. The interesting one is what happens to everyone else.
  The analyst was never primarily a query-writer. The job was converting questions the business couldn't answer itself into answers it could trust. The SQL was the toll you paid to reach the thinking — not the thinking.
  That job got concentrated in one person for one reason: asking was expensive.
  Expensive things get rationed. A queue forms. A queue needs an owner. The analyst is what a question queue looks like when it has a name on it.
  Cheap asking inverts that. The work doesn't vanish — it spreads. The thing only one person could do becomes something the whole team does.
  The pushback writes itself: analysts do judgment, not just queries. True. But that judgment doesn't disappear — it spreads to whoever is now asking. Cheap asking is how non-analysts become analytical.

  What gets scarce isn't analyst time anymore. It's curiosity — knowing what to ask, and caring enough to ask it.
  Make asking free, and everyone becomes the analyst.

  Everyone is talking about AI replacing analysts. The more interesting question is which parts of the job it is already changing.
   
  Yu Dong's "How AI Is Rewriting the Day-to-Day of Data Scientists" takes a practical look. Working as a data scientist, Dong breaks the impact into two categories: eliminating low-value work and accelerating high-value work.
   
  On the low-value side, conversational business intelligence tools and chat-based interfaces are reducing the volume of simple data pull requests that clog analyst queues. On the high-value side, coding assistants, automated exploratory data analysis, and large language model-powered summarization are compressing how long it takes to get from raw data to a first draft of findings.
