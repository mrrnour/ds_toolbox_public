Statistical significance is not the same as practical significance. It only tells if something is "statistically detectable" (or discernible).
This is why in clinical trials, where the final decision on drug approval is based on a p-value from appropriate randomized clinical trials, important conditions must be met:
1) the minimal important difference (MID) is embedded into testing through directional null hypothesis like: non-inferiority, clinical superiority and equivalence studies. Once such H0 is rejected, the effect is both statistically discernible and clinically meaningful. So it's purely a domain-importance-driven approach.
2) studies are designed and powered for detecting the desired effect size.
3) studies are randomized and controlled whenever possible. Limited workarounds are used in observational studies
4) the type-1 error is controlled for the primary and key secondary objectives on the study level
5) all key objectives, endpoints, hypotheses, statistical methods (+parameters) of the analysis and inference, thresholds, policy for outliers and missing data - are planned before the data are seen.
I describe it in my longer (informal) article: Embedding clinical importance in the frequentist hypothesis testing in randomised controlled clinical trials on Substack: [https://lnkd.in/gFaBfWJ9](https://www.linkedin.com/redir/redirect?url=https%3A%2F%2Flnkd%2Ein%2FgFaBfWJ9&urlhash=EXiF&trk=public_post-text)

PS: a few notes ad 5)
Of course I can imagine situations where some planned method failed because of unmet statistical assumptions or some numerical issues/instability, excessive distributional issues, missing data, unexpected excessive number of outliers, some difficult to explain patterns found, and other cases. This is why I always highlight the need for planning alternative analytical scenarios. So, when plan A fails, we have plan B - and maybe plan C (at least in terms of sensitivity analyses). When real trouble happens we don't get stuck and -maybe- the study is saved, because we have alternatives allowing us to move forward. But even the best plans can fail, something unexpected may happen - actually I have been facing it many times, so ad hoc remedies are required do not doom the entire study. BUT! This is why honest SAR (statistical analysis report) for the CSR should clearly address all deviations from the SAP with thorough justification and the discussion of potential outcomes! Similarly, the SAP should outline all deviations from the study protocol. Planning is essential but real life can be very surprising. If we decide to remedy post hoc - it must be made maximally transparent. Only this builds trust.

  An AI model should not turn clues into a diagnosis.
  In one clinical abstraction workflow, the task was to
  identify only the confirmed, current diagnoses
  documented in the note being reviewed.
  But a model saw an elevated A1c and metformin, then
  generated diabetes mellitus.
  The note did not document diabetes.
  It did not list diabetes as a possible or differential
  diagnosis.
  It did not contain the clinical context needed to
  establish that diagnosis.
  That is not extraction. It is unsupported inference.
  A medication may have multiple indications. A lab result
  may be clinically relevant, but it is not self-interpreting.
  Even when several data points appear related, they do
  not automatically establish a diagnosis in the specific
  source note.
  Unless a task explicitly asks for diagnostic-support or
  differential-diagnosis reasoning, the output should
  capture what the documentation states—not fill gaps
  with what a model believes might be true.
  That is why shared guidelines matter across clinical
  informatics, annotation, abstraction, QA, and
  engineering: define the task, define the evidence rules,
  and validate outputs against the actual record.
  The note establishes what it establishes. A model should
  not infer what is not there.

  Moving faster is not progress if we reach the wrong answer sooner.
  That is why the goal should be faster learning, instead of faster clinical trials.
  This is an important distinction.
  Operation TrialBlazer is about accelerating early clinical research and development. But the roadmap does not simply say, “move faster.”
  It also emphasizes something equally important:
  better-designed trials
  adequately powered studies
  stronger rigor
  better go/no-go decisions
  real-world data
  causal inference
  adaptive designs
  master protocols
  This matters because acceleration without rigor can be dangerous.
  It can produce faster noise.
  Faster false confidence.
  Faster expensive failures.
  Faster studies that do not answer the question they were supposed to answer.
  In drug development, speed is valuable only when it improves the rate at which we generate reliable evidence.
  That is also how I think about AI for science.
  AI should not only help us move faster.
  It should help us ask better questions, select better patients, design better protocols, use prior knowledge more intelligently, and learn more from every experiment and every trial.
  A faster clinical trial system should not mean weaker evidence.
  It should mean:
  fewer unnecessary delays
  clearer regulatory expectations
  better patient-trial matching
  more efficient trial designs
  stronger use of existing data
  more transparent decision-making
  better evidence generated per dollar and per patient
  This is where AI, structured knowledge, and clinical research infrastructure come together.
  AI can help identify who should be enrolled.
  Knowledge graphs can help connect mechanisms, biomarkers, diseases, drugs, protocols, and outcomes.
  Real-world data can help assess feasibility and context.
  Causal inference can help separate signal from bias.
  Adaptive and master protocols can make trials more efficient without wasting scientific opportunity.
  But the principle must remain clear:
  The purpose of speed is not to skip rigor.
  The purpose of speed is to learn the truth sooner.
  That is the clinical trial system we should be building.

  [Viraj Narayanan](https://www.linkedin.com/in/virajnarayanan?trk=public_post-text) is saying something that needs to be said more loudly in life sciences: LLMs are not the hard part. The data underneath them is. Because without the data, the LLMs are irrelevant.
  Our industry gets excited about the model. It's the shiny object. But the reality on the ground — the one Viraj describes from rooms with executives and analysts — is that 70% of life sciences teams spend six or more months wrestling data into shape before they can derive a single insight. The model is the easy part. Getting there is not.
  I'd add one layer to his framing though. "AI-ready" is actually two distinct things that are getting conflated.
  The first is what Cornerstone does — qualification, normalization, harmonization. Getting data clean, integrated, and trustworthy. Viraj puts a fine point on it: the same adverse event coded three different ways across three studies will silently corrupt any cross-trial reasoning built on top of it. That is foundational, genuinely hard, and almost always underestimated.
  But here's what that work unlocks — and this is the part that isn't being talked about yet.
  Once your data is clean, you haven't arrived at "AI-ready." You've arrived at Judgment Ready Data.
  Judgment Ready Data is knowing which data to use for which question, what model fits what data, what biases exist, what the data can and can't answer. And getting there is its own hard problem — legacy systems, infrastructure, and platforms were never built for a world where AI judgment is the end goal.
  The organizations that win won't just have clean data. They'll have people who know what to do with it — and systems built from the ground up for that purpose. The companies building those systems, the infrastructure for Judgment Ready Data, are the ones worth watching.
