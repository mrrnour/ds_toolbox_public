A/B Testing: the parts most teams skip.
- Define sample size before you start. Your expected effect size determines how many observations you need. Without this, you have no principled stopping criterion.
- p-value ≠ probability that B is better. It's the probability of seeing your data if there were no effect. Bayesian testing gives you what you actually want — the direct probability that B beats A, with a full posterior distribution.
- One variable per experiment. Change two things, and your result is unattributable. You get a winner but no transferable insight.
- Optimize the right metric. Click-through rate is a proxy. If the business runs on revenue or retention, that's what the experiment should measure.
- Read the credible interval, not just the lift. A +1.47pp median lift with CI [+0.8, +2.2] is a different decision than one whose interval crosses zero. The uncertainty matters as much as the estimate.
Losing variants are worth documenting. A well-understood null result informs the next hypothesis!

  180 users. No verified population variance.
  Three months later, the decision was reversed.
  Here's what nobody explains clearly:
  Z-test vs T-test is not a style choice. It comes down to one question.
  Do you actually know the true population standard deviation?
  → Z-test: use when you know σ (the real population standard deviation). Almost never true in data science work.
  → T-test: use when you're estimating it from your sample. This is real life.
  The dangerous myth your stats course taught you:
  "If n > 30, switch to Z-test."
  Not a law. That shortcut exists because large samples make T-distribution approximate Z.
  But the T-test still works perfectly at large n. There's zero downside.
  Using Z on a small sample with unknown variance? Your confidence intervals are lying to you.
  In practice, ask yourself: do I have documented, verified population-level σ?
  → Product experiments? T-test.
  → Model performance comparisons? T-test.
  → Customer behavior across segments? T-test.
  You'd use Z-test in manufacturing with decades of process data, or standardized testing with established population norms.
  Not in a product analytics team.
  The test choice is the analysis. The computation after it is just arithmetic.
  Know your assumptions before you run anything.

  Agents are just probability with a personality.
  I’ve enjoyed co-designing data models, content specs, and system prompts for the agent we’re building in an attempt to make its output predictable:
  – the data model decides what’s sayable
  – the content spec decides what “good” looks like
  – the system prompt decides how it should think about the job
  Regardless of how bulletproof you think your context is, you get something that’s mostly right, occasionally brilliant, and sometimes confidently wrong in a way that makes you want to bang your head against the wall.
  A few things I learnt to follow:
  1. Examples beat instructions. Showing the model 2–3 samples of a good output does more than 200 words describing one.
  2. Constrain the shape, not just the content. Use ASCII wireframes, JSON schemas to force the output into a structure shrinking the failure surface.
  3. Decide what the human owns. Human-in-the-loop is an intentional design choice about which steps the agent should never own in the first place.
  More instructions don’t result in more control but more ambiguity. Past a point, you’re just giving the model more rope to misinterpret.
  The better goal is designing for legible failure instead of perfect success. If I can’t prevent a weird output, I want it weird in a way I can see, catch, and route around.

  The follow-up question is where scenario work gets interesting.
  The first request is normally manageable, even if it takes a bit of effort.
  You pull the numbers together, make a few assumptions, update the model, sense-check the answer, and get something back to the team.
  Then someone replies with:
  “Great - but what if we looked at it another way?”
  That is usually the moment where the work changes, you uncover the complexity and hidden logic from the initial request.
  It stops being a simple scenario and becomes a much bigger question about how the model is built, which assumptions are connected/available and how high the quality of the data is we will have to scrape together to answer this.
  I’ve been caught in that loop plenty of times.
  The first version gives people confidence. The follow-up questions are where you find out how much confidence the model actually deserves.
  What was the follow-up question that sent your model sideways?

  Raw data loses value. A click or a view means something today. In six months, it means nothing.
  Many teams collect too much data. They have charts but no clarity. They make decisions based on memory or opinion. This makes software work hard to evaluate.
  Add context to your events. Include these details:
  - Workflow name
  - Outcome state
  - Source
  - Release context
  - Retention rule
  Do not track every action. Focus on signals. Signals help you act with confidence.
  Name your workflows. Keep outcomes visible. Show uncertainty.
  Test your data. Ask this:
  Does a new person understand this event without asking you?
  If the answer is no, your data is incomplete.
  Good systems explain their own state. They show what happened. They show what changed. They tell you which decision comes next.
  Stop chasing more data. Focus on the right signal.

  The Confusion Matrix Explained — Finally, Simply
  Your model shows 95% accuracy. You ship it. It fails in production.
  Here's what happened: your dataset had 95% negative samples. The model learned to predict "No" for everything.
  And you called it accurate.
  This is why the confusion matrix exists.
  Four values you must know:
  → True Positive: Model said Yes. It was Yes.
  → True Negative: Model said No. It was No.
  → False Positive: Model said Yes. It was No. (Type I error)
  → False Negative: Model said No. It was Yes. (Type II error)
  From these four, three metrics matter most:
  Precision = TP / (TP + FP) → How often is your "Yes" correct?
  Recall = TP / (TP + FN) → How many real "Yes" cases did you catch?
  F1 Score = Harmonic mean of both
  In fraud detection, a false negative is catastrophic.
  In spam filters, a false positive is annoying.
  Choose your metric based on what failure costs more.
  Accuracy is a vanity metric. Use it wisely.

  Association is not explanation.
  Two variables moving together does not mean one drives the other. Correlation summarizes patterns in data. It does not reveal mechanism.
  Explanation requires structure. What is the driver? Through which channel? Under what constraints?
  Without that discipline, models become descriptive. They fit historical co-movements and appear precise. But when regimes shift, the relationship breaks.
  In macro and financial systems, agents adapt. Policies change. Incentives evolve. Pure association assumes stability.
  Understanding requires causal assumptions that can be tested, stressed, and revised.
  Association tells us what happened.
  Explanation tells us why.
  If robustness matters, we cannot stop at patterns.
