The misconception about ranked tests comparing medians is so widespread so it needs to be spoken louder: 🚫 rank based tests don't compare medians or even entire distributions in general. I explain it with examples and valuable books and papers in my post on the Research gate: [https://lnkd.in/d\_wC-quu](https://www.linkedin.com/redir/redirect?url=https%3A%2F%2Flnkd%2Ein%2Fd_wC-quu&urlhash=mE4q&trk=public_post-text)
If one \*truly wants\* to compare means under non-normal distributions - the permutation Welch t test is the solution.
[https://lnkd.in/dYxPhfaZ](https://www.linkedin.com/redir/redirect?url=https%3A%2F%2Flnkd%2Ein%2FdYxPhfaZ&urlhash=279q&trk=public_post-text)
For comparing medians - quantile regression. For paired medians - mixed-effect quantile regression.

Every data professional forgets which statistical test to use. Here's the fix.
(Bookmark it. Seriously.)
I've been there:
↳ Staring at two datasets wondering which test to run
↳ Googling "t-test vs ANOVA" for the 10th time
↳ Second-guessing myself in an interview
Choosing the wrong statistical test can invalidate your findings and lead to flawed conclusions.
Here's your quick reference guide:
𝐂𝐨𝐦𝐩𝐚𝐫𝐢𝐧𝐠 𝐌𝐞𝐚𝐧𝐬:
↳ 2 independent groups → Independent t-Test
↳ Same group, before/after → Paired t-Test
↳ 3+ groups → ANOVA
𝐍𝐨𝐧-𝐍𝐨𝐫𝐦𝐚𝐥 𝐃𝐚𝐭𝐚:
↳ 2 groups → Mann-Whitney U Test
↳ Paired samples → Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test
↳ 3+ groups → Kruskal-Wallis Test
𝐑𝐞𝐥𝐚𝐭𝐢𝐨𝐧𝐬𝐡𝐢𝐩𝐬:
↳ Linear relationship → Pearson Correlation
↳ Ranked/non-linear → Spearman Correlation
↳ Two categorical variables → Chi-Square Test
𝐏𝐫𝐞𝐝𝐢𝐜𝐭𝐢𝐨𝐧:
↳ Continuous outcome → Linear Regression
↳ Binary outcome (yes/no) → Logistic Regression
𝐕𝐚𝐫𝐢𝐚𝐧𝐜𝐞:
↳ Compare spread between groups → Levene's Test / F-Test

  🧹 Raw data is never clean data. Here's proof.
  I recently worked on a customer churn dataset with 10,300 rows. Before any analysis or modeling could happen, I had to fix it.
  Here's what I found hiding in the data:
  ❌ 300 fully duplicated rows — silently inflating the dataset
  ⚠️ Inconsistent casing across Region and Subscription\_Plan ("south" vs "South", "BASIC" vs "Basic")
  👤 990 rows with "Unknown" gender — not a category, a missing value in disguise
  📉 Negative ages. A customer aged 150. Yes, really.
  💸 Monthly spend values up to 99,999 — extreme outliers skewing every metric
  🔢 2,272 missing values across Age, Monthly\_Spend, and Payment\_Method
  After cleaning:
  ✅ 10,000 quality rows
  ✅ Zero nulls
  ✅ No outliers
  ✅ Consistent formatting throughout
  Data cleaning isn't glamorous. But skipping it means your models learn from noise, not signal.
  The best analysts don't just run models — they ask "can I trust this data?" first.

  Data Science Questions of the Day (16/30) -
  1. How do you calculate sample size for an A/B test?
  Sample size depends on:
  • Baseline metric
  • Confidence level
  • Statistical power
  • MDE (expected improvement)
  Detailed Process here:[https://lnkd.in/gYFmQQpy](https://www.linkedin.com/redir/redirect?url=https%3A%2F%2Flnkd%2Ein%2FgYFmQQpy&urlhash=B__x&trk=public_post-text)
  2. What is statistical power and why is it important?
  Statistical Power = Probability of detecting a real effect.
  • Common target: 80%
  • Higher power → Lower chance of missing true improvements
  3. What is Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)?
  Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) is a critical concept in any type of testing. It represents the smallest improvement (uplift) you want to be able to confidently detect as statistically significant in a test metric. In essence, MDE defines the sensitivity of your experiment.
  Why it matters: It determines the sample size needed. Smaller effects are harder to detect and require more data (samples) compared to larger effects.
  4. Statistical significance vs Practical significance
  Statistical significance tells you if a result is mathematically real and not just due to random chance.
  Practical significance asks whether that difference is actually large enough to matter in the real world.
  Statistical significance is about evidence, while practical significance is about impact.
  To make sound decisions, professionals usually evaluate both metrics together:
  First, check statistical significance. If your result is mathematically reliable, you know the effect isn't just noise.
  Next, measure the effect size. Use Effect Size Calculators to determine the absolute magnitude of the difference.
  Apply domain expertise. Decide if the observed difference justifies the cost, time, or resources required to implement the change.
  5. What are Type I and Type II errors?
  Type I Error (False Positive)
  • Detecting an effect that doesn't exist.
  Type II Error (False Negative)
  • Missing a real effect.
  Tradeoff: Reducing one often increases the other.
  6. Why is randomization important in A/B testing?
  Randomization:
  • Reduces bias
  • Creates comparable groups
  • Improves experiment validity
  Goal: Ensure observed differences come from the experiment, not external factors.
  Key Takeaway: Reliable A/B tests require the right sample size, sufficient power, a meaningful MDE, and proper randomization. Always evaluate both statistical and business impact before making decisions.

  We don’t need more data. We need better sampling🙋♀️🙋♂️
  Better sampling comes when we:
  🔍 Step 1: Start with the decision—not the data
  Before you even think about sample size, ask:
  👉 What decision will this data inform?
  Your sampling strategy should serve that decision.
  Not the other way around.
  🎯 Step 2: Define your population clearly
  Who should be represented?
  Not just “beneficiaries”—but:
  • New vs. long-term participants
  • Regular vs. irregular attendees
  • Included vs. excluded groups
  This is where most sampling bias quietly begins.
  ⚖️ Step 3: Choose the right approach (and how to actually do it)
  1. Random Sampling
  Best for: generalizable, unbiased estimates
  How:
  • Build a full list of your population (sampling frame)
  • Assign each unit a number
  • Use a random generator (Excel, Python, etc.) to select your sample
  2. Stratified Sampling
  Best for: ensuring representation across key groups
  How:
  • Divide your population into meaningful strata (e.g., gender, region)
  • Decide sample size for each group (proportional or equal)
  • Randomly sample within each stratum
  3. Cluster Sampling
  Best for: large or geographically dispersed populations
  How:
  • Divide population into clusters (e.g., villages, schools)
  • Randomly select a subset of clusters
  • Survey all—or a random sample—within selected clusters
  4. Purposive Sampling
  Best for: deep insights, qualitative exploration
  How:
  • Define clear selection criteria (e.g., dropouts, high performers)
  • Intentionally select participants who meet those criteria
  • Ensure variation to capture different perspectives
  5. Convenience Sampling
  Best for: speed and access
  How:
  • Collect data from those who are easiest to reach
  • Be transparent about limitations
  • Use only when constraints are real—not just comfortable
  🔁 Step 4: Stress-test your sample
  Ask yourself:
  👉 Who is missing from this sample?
  👉 Whose reality might look different?
  If the answer is “we don’t know”—that’s your signal.
  📊 Step 5: Document your choices
  Sampling is full of trade-offs.
  Be explicit about them.
  I’ve seen entire program conclusions shift
  just by including the voices we initially overlooked.
  So next time you design a study, ask:
  👉 Are we sampling for accuracy… or for convenience?

  Decisions under uncertainty. That's it.
  Everything else is a tactic.
  We wrap data analytics in dashboards, models, pipelines, slick charts. Strip all of it away and one job remains: shrink the uncertainty around a decision someone actually has to make.
  A dashboard nobody acts on is decoration. A model that changes no behaviour is a hobby. A 40-slide report that ends in "let's keep monitoring" is procrastination in a suit.
  So before I open a dataset now, I ask two questions: What decision is this for? And what would change my mind?
  If I can't answer both, I don't start.
  The hard part of analytics was never the maths. It's the discipline to refuse the analysis nobody will use.
  What's the hardest call you've had to make on incomplete data?
